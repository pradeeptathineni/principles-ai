# Teaching Tutoring

This file covers direct tutoring interactions with one learner or a small group. Program selection, classroom-wide instruction, curriculum design, assessment policy, and safeguarding remain in their own teaching subdomains.

## Principles

### PRN-000005 — Diagnose before you direct

Begin from evidence of what the learner currently understands, then adapt the session to the specific gap or misconception instead of following a fixed explanation. [1], [2]

#### Why

Tutoring creates an opportunity for responsive instruction, but close interaction does not guarantee accurate diagnosis. Observational research found tutors sometimes ignored confusion or followed their own plans, while practical syntheses recommend ongoing diagnostic assessment to target content and challenge. [1], [2]

#### Practices

- Ask the learner to attempt a representative task and explain each step before intervening. [1], [2]
- Use errors, hesitations, and questions to distinguish a missing prerequisite, a misconception, and an execution slip; choose the next task or explanation accordingly. [1], [2]
- Recheck understanding with a new prompt after intervening; do not rely on “Do you understand?” as the main evidence. [1], [2]

### PRN-000006 — Make the learner generate the reasoning

Structure the dialogue so the learner predicts, explains, justifies, and revises rather than merely listening to the tutor's reasoning. [2], [3]

#### Why

In research on human tutoring, learners' constructive responses and reflection were more closely associated with learning than tutor talk alone. Broader evidence syntheses also identify self-explanation and teaching as promising strategies because they require learners to retrieve, organize, and integrate knowledge. [2], [3]

#### Practices

- Ask for a prediction, explanation, or proposed next step before supplying your own. [2]
- Prompt the learner to compare strategies, justify a choice, and repair an error; withhold your explanation long enough for a substantive response. [2]
- After explaining, return the cognitive work to the learner by asking them to apply the idea to a different case. [2], [3]

### PRN-000007 — Calibrate help, then withdraw it

Give enough support to make productive progress possible, then reduce that support as competence grows so the learner performs an increasing share of the task. [4], [5]

#### Why

Scaffolding can let a learner work on task elements within reach while the tutor temporarily manages harder elements. Too little help can leave the learner floundering, while too much can suppress generation and create dependence; research does not support one universal assistance level for every learner and task. [4], [5]

#### Practices

- Start with a likely sufficient support—a goal reminder, cue, partial step, or worked model—and escalate when the learner's attempts show that it is insufficient. [4], [5]
- After an assisted success, remove part of the support and ask the learner to continue or solve a parallel task. [4]
- Watch for both repeated unproductive attempts and help-seeking that replaces thinking; adjust assistance from observed performance rather than a fixed tutoring script. [4], [5]

#### Applicability

Novices and complex tasks may require more explicit guidance, while greater competence can support lighter intervention. Treat the balance as an evidence-guided judgment, not a rule that minimal help is always best. [4], [5]

### PRN-000008 — Make feedback produce a next move

Give feedback about the task, process, or self-regulation that identifies a meaningful gap and directs the learner's next action; avoid feedback centered on vague personal praise. [6]

#### Why

Feedback can improve or impair learning depending on its focus and use. It is most informative when it connects current performance to a goal and helps the learner close the gap; comments about the person are generally less useful than information about the work, strategy, or self-monitoring. [6]

#### Practices

- Name what is working, pinpoint the earliest consequential gap, and connect it to a clear goal or criterion. [6]
- Prefer a cue or question the learner can use to revise; supply instruction directly when missing knowledge means feedback alone cannot work. [2], [6]
- Require an observable response to feedback, such as a revision, explanation, or another attempt. [6]

### PRN-000009 — Check learning without the tutor

Reserve an unassisted check on a representative or varied task, and use its result to plan later retrieval rather than treating assisted completion as mastery. [3], [4]

#### Why

Assisted success can show what a learner can recognize or complete with scaffolding, not yet what they can produce independently. Unassisted retrieval provides different evidence, and later retrieval and varied practice strengthen access beyond the immediate session. [3], [4]

#### Practices

- End with a closed-notes explanation or problem completed without hints; if the learner stalls, treat that result as diagnostic evidence for the next teaching move. [3], [4]
- Revisit important material after an interval through retrieval, and vary examples when the learner needs to recognize the idea in new contexts. [3]
- Record the next target from the unassisted response and align future practice with the learner's curriculum or stated goal. [1], [3]

#### Applicability

For very new material, an unassisted attempt may diagnose readiness rather than demonstrate mastery. Interpret the attempt as information, not as a penalty or proof of inability. [3], [5]
